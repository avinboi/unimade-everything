The hard truth about the ceiling

One campus of laundry is a small business: about 200 to 300 subscribers, or ₹2 to 3 lakh of monthly revenue and ₹60,000 to ₹1 lakh of monthly profit. That is a very good student business and an excellent learning project. It becomes a company only if you prove you can copy the model to campus #2, #3 and #10 with the same economics. That's what investors look for: "density" (many customers per square kilometre) and "replicability" (the playbook works anywhere). So the goal for year one is not "become the student super-app". It's "prove one campus works, write the playbook, then copy it."

Why laundry, of all seven services?

  • Highest frequency: every student, every week. Storage is twice a year; internships aren't something students pay for.
  • Easiest to subscribe to: predictable volume (a bag a week), so a monthly pass makes sense to the customer and gives you recurring revenue.
  • You don't need a kitchen, a licence or inventory (unlike food, which needs FSSAI registration, hygiene control, and competes with Swiggy/Zomato).
  • It earns you the right to sell more. A laundry runner visits the same hostels twice a week. That trust and that route are what you later use to sell cleaning, storage and kits.
  • The laundry bag is a media channel. Your bag goes into hundreds of rooms. Later, brands that want to reach students (snacks, deodorant, apps) can pay to put samples or flyers in it.

The pricing problem, in numbers

Your newsletter says: "Service fee ₹50/order · delivery ₹30/order · partner commission stays on the vendor side. Unimade+ is ₹199/month."

A student compares ₹{{current_pricing.total}} with the dhobi down the road and walks away. Three other problems with this model:

  • "Partner commission on the vendor side" means asking a local laundry shop or tiffin uncle to give you 15–20% of every order. Small vendors rarely agree, and when they do, they raise prices or cut quality, and your brand takes the blame.
  • You don't control the experience. In a marketplace, the vendor decides quality and timing. In D2C, you own the customer and the experience. That is the whole point of a brand.
  • Unimade+ at ₹199/month doesn't say what the student gets. People pay for outcomes ("your laundry, done, every week"), not for memberships.

The fix: one simple all-inclusive price, where you buy laundry wholesale from a partner and sell it under your brand. "₹{{laundry.inputs.price}}/month. 4 pickups. Done." No fees, no surprises. Section 7 shows the maths.

4. The customer and the market

Who exactly is the customer?

Session 1 warns: "Every word must be specific. No 'everyone.'" So:

Primary customer (the user): a 1st or 2nd-year undergraduate living away from home in a hostel or PG near campus, in your Gurugram cluster. Has class 9-to-5, no washing machine in the room, or a shared machine with a queue. Gets ₹8,000–20,000 a month from parents for living costs estimate.

Secondary customer (often the payer): their parent. Worried their child isn't eating or living properly, happy to pay for something that makes life easier. Your poster "Mom's not here. But we are." speaks to exactly this person. Parents buy the Starter Kit and can gift the Laundry Pass.

Later customer (B2B): PG owners who want to offer laundry as part of rent without running it themselves. One PG owner can mean 30–100 students at once.

The problem, in one sentence (Session 1 format)

"For hostel and PG students who lose hours every week to laundry, Unimade is a laundry subscription that picks up from your room and returns it folded in 48 hours. Unlike the local dhobi or the hostel machine, Unimade is booked on WhatsApp, tracked, priced up front, and your clothes are never mixed with anyone else's."

Short version: "We help hostel students get their laundry done without thinking about it, by picking it up from their room every week."

Seasonality: the thing that will surprise you

Students leave in vacations. Your laundry business will nearly stop in mid-May to July and drop in December (exams and winter break). The plan handles this in two ways: the Starter Kit sells hardest in exactly those months (freshers buy in June to August), and the Pass is priced per month with an easy pause, so students don't cancel forever when they go home.

The "student OS" vision isn't dead. It's sequenced.

Every big "super-app" started with one thing done extremely well: Swiggy was food delivery, Urban Company started with a few home services, Zomato was restaurant menus. Each step uses the customers and trust from the last one.

Pricing psychology: why these exact numbers

  • ₹999, not ₹1,000: it reads as "under a thousand", and an easy "₹250 a week" story.
  • Per month, not per kg: students hate calculating. "4 pickups a month, done" is simple. Per-kg pricing makes them weigh every T-shirt.
  • ₹99 first pickup, not free: "free" brings people who never intended to pay. ₹99 filters for real interest, and shows they'll pay at all.
  • Single pickup at ₹{{ppp.inputs.price}} makes the Pass look like a deal: 4 single pickups would cost ₹1,196 vs ₹{{laundry.inputs.price}}.
  • Semester Pass: later, offer 5 months for ₹4,499 (pay upfront, ~10% off). It gives you cash in advance and fewer cancellations.
  • Never discount the Pass below cost to grow faster. You'd be buying customers who leave when the discount ends.

How money flows

Student pays Unimade up front (Razorpay link / UPI) → Unimade pays the laundromat weekly for kg processed → Unimade pays the runner per pickup or a monthly salary. You collect first and pay later. That's healthy for cash flow. For kits it's the opposite: you buy stock before you sell it, which is why the cash plan in Section 9 matters, and why pre-orders are the key tactic for the kit.

11. Operations: how it actually gets done

For a service brand, operations is the product. One lost T-shirt shared in a hostel WhatsApp group can undo a month of marketing.

Choosing the laundry partner

  • Visit 5 laundromats within 3 km. Ask for a bulk per-kg rate for 200–500 kg a month, with each customer's bag washed separately (script in the Appendix).
  • Test 3 of them with your own and friends' clothes for 2 weeks. Check: turnaround, colour bleeding, folding, missing items.
  • Sign a one-page agreement: rate, 24–36 hour turnaround, separate washing, damage responsibility, weekly payment.
  • Keep a backup partner. Never depend on one shop.

The weekly flow (no app needed)

  1. Booking: subscribers are auto-scheduled for their hostel's slot; single pickups book on WhatsApp.
  2. Pickup: runner collects tagged Unimade bags (name, room, phone, order ID) at fixed times, photographs each sealed bag.
  3. Weigh and count: at the partner, each bag is weighed and pieces counted; the count goes back to the student on WhatsApp ("12 pieces, 4.2 kg received").
  4. Wash: separately per bag, within 24–36 hours.
  5. Return: folded, in a poly bag with the tag; delivered to the room or reception; "Delivered ✓" on WhatsApp.
  6. Feedback: one-tap rating after each return; any complaint answered within 2 hours.

Don't build an app yet

Kit operations

  • Sourcing: Delhi's Sadar Bazaar and IndiaMART suppliers. Get 3 quotes per item, order samples, build 5 test kits and show them to 20 students and parents before buying in bulk.
  • Electricals must be BIS-certified (extension board, lamp). Never source unmarked electricals.
  • Batches: buy 30 for the January test; 100 → 150 → 200 for the summer, re-ordering only as stock sells. Pre-orders first.
  • Packing and storage: a small rented store room or a friend's garage; pack in batches of 50 with a checklist per box.
  • Delivery: hand delivery to campus hostels on move-in day (cheapest, best experience); courier (Shiprocket-type aggregators) for other cities.

GST in plain words

  • Services (laundry): registration is required once yearly turnover crosses ₹20 lakh. Your laundry business stays below that in year one.
  • Goods (kit): the general threshold is ₹40 lakh, but selling on Amazon/Flipkart needs GST from the first sale (S16). Selling across states, or through marketplaces, usually triggers registration. Check your exact case with a CA.

The team question (the most important page in this section)

Your website lists five team members; you say only you are working now. If Unimade succeeds and nothing is written down, any of them can later claim a share, and arguments between co-founders kill more startups than competitors do. Fix it now, while it's cheap and friendly:

  1. Have one honest conversation with each person: "I'm going full force on this. Are you in, with X hours a week, for the next 6 months?"
  2. Equity follows future work, not past attendance. People who step back can get a small thank-you share (e.g. 1–2%) or nothing, agreed in writing.
  3. Use vesting: shares are earned over 4 years with a 1-year "cliff" (someone who leaves before a year gets nothing). This is standard and protects everyone.
  4. Write a one-page founders' agreement now (roles, equity, vesting, decision-making, what happens if someone leaves). Formalise it when you register a company.
  5. Update the website to show the real team.

Practical roles if 1–2 people stay: you on partners, operations and numbers; a second person on content, community and the kit. Add part-time campus ambassadors (paid in Passes plus ₹50 per subscriber they bring) instead of more co-founders.

Money discipline from day one

  • Separate bank account for Unimade. Never mix it with personal money.
  • Record every rupee in and out weekly, with receipts.
  • Pay suppliers on time: your reputation with the laundromat is an asset.
  • Don't pay yourself until the business has 3 months of fixed costs saved in the bank.

16. Things you didn't ask but need to know

  1. Customer service is the brand. In a service business, how you handle a mistake matters more than the mistake. Reply fast, own it, fix it, give something back.
  2. Density beats reach. 50 subscribers in one hostel is a better business than 50 spread across 10. Runner cost per bag falls, word of mouth spreads, and you can say "everyone in Block C uses it."
  3. Retention is the real growth engine. Winning a subscriber costs ₹300; keeping one costs almost nothing. Ask everyone who cancels why, every time.
  4. Vanity metrics lie. Followers, waitlist sign-ups and app "interest" aren't customers. Only count people who've paid.
  5. "Do things that don't scale" first. Do the first 100 pickups yourself. You'll learn what breaks, what students care about, and what to write in the playbook.
  6. Parents are a hidden market. They pay, they worry, and they're on Facebook and WhatsApp, where ads are cheaper. Speak to them directly for the kit and gifted Passes.
  7. Write things down. Every repeated task becomes a one-page SOP (standard operating procedure). That's how you hand work to a runner, an ambassador, or a campus #2 manager.
  8. Price is a signal. Too cheap looks unreliable. ₹999 for "never think about laundry again" is a confident, fair price. Compete on reliability, not on being cheapest.
  9. Collect proof from day one. Photos, reviews, numbers ("2,000 bags washed, 0 lost"). It powers your website, ads, PR, and future investor or incubator pitches.
  10. Your course project and your business are the same thing. Every course deliverable (canvas, ATL sheet, media plan, listing) can use real Unimade data. Your grades and your business both improve.
  11. Keep fixed costs near zero until revenue is real. No office, no app developer, no salaries before demand is proven.
  12. Studies come first in exam weeks. Plan the business calendar around the academic calendar. That's also when your customers are busiest.
  13. Learn to say no. Students will ask, "Can you also do food? Printing? Bike repair?" Write every request down (it's your future roadmap), but say "not yet" until laundry runs without you.

Payback periodMonths to earn back a customer's CAC.
Pre-orderCustomer pays before you buy or make the stock.
ROASReturn on ad spend = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend.
SKUStock keeping unit: one specific product variant.
SMPSingle minded proposition: one promise, one audience, ≤5 words. S17.
SOPStandard operating procedure: a written how-to for a repeated task.
TAM / SAM / SOMTotal / serviceable / obtainable market size.
Unit economicsProfit or loss on one customer or one product.
UTMTags added to links so you can see which campaign a visitor came from.

Model files: the financial model is a small Python script (scripts/model.py). Change any assumption, re-run it, and every number in this report updates.
